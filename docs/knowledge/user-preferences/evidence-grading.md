# Building a user profile: evidence grading

Status: rule set by the owner, 5 Oct 2026. Applies to anything that builds or states a profile of a person (assistant tools, suggestions, "what we know about you").

No personal data belongs in this repo (it is public). This file holds the rule only.

## The rule

Distinguish observed facts from inferred preferences. Do not generalise from one or two examples. For every conclusion, state the evidence strength and how many independent interactions support it. If the evidence is insufficient, say "insufficient evidence" rather than filling the gap.

**Do not turn behaviour into preference unless the person explicitly states the preference, or the same pattern occurs across at least three materially different situations.**

## Grades

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Established | Directly stated by the person, or repeatedly demonstrated. |
| Probable | Supported by several independent examples, not explicitly stated. |
| Tentative | Weak inference from limited examples. |
| Unknown | Not enough information. |

## Before presenting a profile

State first whether the available evidence is sufficient to make one reliably. If it is not, say so, and describe only what the evidence supports.

Opening line, for example:

> Evidence quality: limited. I have several recent cooking interactions, but they are concentrated around a small number of meals and ingredients. They are enough to describe some recent behaviours, but not enough to infer a reliable overall food-preference profile.

## Wording

Say what was observed, not what it means about the person.

- Not: "You prefer Mediterranean food."
- But: "Tentative: several recent meals used yoghurt, feta, cucumber and olive oil. This shows recent use of Mediterranean-style flavours. It does not establish that Mediterranean food is a general preference."

## Why (from the owner)

- What someone cooked recently often reflects what was easy to get, not what they like.
- A profile built that way got things wrong (it listed foods the person would "happily repeat" that they would not).
- People may not yet know what they like. Taste moves through exposure, discovery and appetite, so a thin history is not a taste.
- The person should be able to ask why something was surfaced, and correct it.

## Related, from the same discussion

- Preference is not habit. Keep them apart.
- Severity is separate from preference. A soft avoidance and a hard allergy must never be handled the same way, and neither is inferred from behaviour: both come from what the person states.
- Do not invent dislikes that haven't been stated.

## Open questions

- What counts as "materially different" situations.
- What counts as an "independent interaction" (same day, same session, same meal).
- Where the grade and the count are stored, and how they are shown back to the person.
